# Analytical Research + Scriptwriter

A combined research and scriptwriting skill for analytical football content. Use this when you want to go from a topic idea straight to a publish-ready script in the analytical style — grounded in real data, structured for short-form video, and built around a strong central argument.

---

## What This Skill Does

This skill walks you through two phases in sequence:

1. **Research Phase** — identify what's actually interesting about a topic, find the real argument, and gather the evidence that supports it
2. **Script Phase** — structure that evidence into a short-form analytical video script (60–120s) with a hook, evidence stack, and decisive payoff

The output is a clean, spoken-word-only script ready to record — no camera directions, no stage cues, just the words.

## Phase 1 — Research Framework

Before writing a single line of script, Claude should do the following:

### Step 1 — Find the Real Argument
For any given topic, the surface-level take is not the script. Ask:
- What does conventional wisdom say about this topic?
- What does the data actually show — is it more nuanced, the opposite, or more extreme?
- What is the most interesting, specific, defensible claim you can make about this topic?
- Can it be stated in one sentence with no hedging?

**Output: One sentence. The central claim.** If it can't be stated in one clean sentence, the argument isn't sharp enough yet.

### Step 2 — Gather the Evidence Beats
Identify 2–4 specific, concrete pieces of evidence that support the claim. Each piece must be:
- **Specific:** a real stat, a named player, a real match with a date or scoreline
- **Relevant:** directly proves or advances the central claim
- **Ordered:** arranged from foundational → escalating → most impactful

For each evidence beat, note:
- What the fact is
- Why it supports the claim
- What comparison or context makes it meaningful

### Step 3 — Find the Complication
Identify the single strongest counter-argument. This is not the same as finding reasons you might be wrong — it's finding the most credible objection a sceptical viewer would raise. Name it directly. The script will address it and explain why it doesn't change the conclusion.

### Step 4 — Define the "Why Now"
Connect the topic to something current: an ongoing league, a transfer window, a recent match, a live debate. Without this, the video feels like an essay. With it, it feels urgent.

## Phase 2 — Script Structure

Once research is complete, write the script using this architecture:

### Beat 1 — Hook (0–5 seconds)
One sentence. A bold claim, a surprising stat, or a counter-intuitive observation. No intro, no name-drop, no "today we're looking at." The hook is the argument, compressed.

Choose from these hook types based on topic:
- **Contrarian** for rankings, player debates, GOAT arguments: *"[Popular take] — and it's wrong."*
- **Curiosity** for systems, tactical breakdowns, "why" formats: *"When [player] does [thing], there's a hidden reason nobody talks about."*
- **Shock** for incidents, stats, extreme outcomes: *"[Team/player] [extreme thing]. Here's exactly how it happened."*

### Beat 2 — Frame (5–15 seconds)
Why does this matter now? One or two sentences connecting the topic to something live. Establish who we're talking about and what the conventional view is — so you can challenge it.

### Beat 3 — Evidence Stack (15s–75% of runtime)
Deliver 2–4 evidence beats. Structure each one:
1. State the point (one declarative sentence)
2. Support with one specific fact, stat, or example
3. Connect back to the central claim (one sentence)
Then move immediately to the next beat. No padding, no repetition.

The staircase rule: each beat must make the argument stronger than the one before it. If beat 3 feels like a repeat of beat 1, cut it.

### Beat 4 — The Complication (~65% mark)
Name the best counter-argument in one sentence. Address it in one or two sentences. Then use it to sharpen the conclusion: "Which actually makes the case stronger, because..."

This is what separates analysis from hot takes. Do not skip it.

### Beat 5 — Payoff (75–90% of runtime)
Restate the central claim — but now it's earned. It should feel inevitable. Add one sentence of implication: what this means going forward, for the player, team, or viewer's own understanding.

### Beat 6 — Close (final 10%)
Choose one:
- **Comments:** An open question that invites disagreement or adds stakes. "So the only real question is: [genuine debate question]?"
- **Follow:** A soft category line. "For more breakdowns like this, follow." Only once. Never mid-script.

## Voice & Delivery Style

This is analytical content. The voice is:

**Informed and confident.** You have done the work. State claims as facts, not suggestions. No "I think," no "you could argue," no "maybe." If the evidence supports the claim, say it directly.

**Precise.** Use exact numbers, named players, real matches. "His xG in away games drops 31%" is analytical. "He doesn't perform away from home" is a pub opinion.

**Efficient.** Every sentence advances the argument. No decorative phrases, no scene-setting beyond what's needed, no repetition of a point already made. If a sentence doesn't move the argument forward, cut it.

**Accessible.** Define jargon the first time you use it — one clause, immediately after the term. Never assume the viewer knows what pressing intensity or xG90 means. Define and move on.

**Decisive.** The payoff must land with a clear verdict. Do not end on "so there are arguments on both sides." Make the call.

## Word Count / Length Guide

| Target video | Target word count | Beats to include |
|---|---|---|
| 60s | 140–160 words | Hook, Frame, 2 evidence beats, Payoff, Close |
| 90s | 200–240 words | Hook, Frame, 3 evidence beats, Complication, Payoff, Close |
| 120s | 280–330 words | Hook, Frame, 4 evidence beats, Complication, Payoff, Close |

Read aloud at natural conversational pace before recording. If it runs more than 10% over the target length, cut the weakest evidence beat — not the payoff or complication.

## Topics This Skill Works Best For

- Player analysis (specific attribute, big-game performance, statistical outlier)
- Tactical breakdowns (a system, a pressing shape, why a team succeeds/fails)
- "Why" explainers (why a team is underperforming, why a transfer makes or doesn't make sense)
- Rankings and comparisons (with defined criteria — never just "who's better")
- Deep dives (career inflection points, manager decisions, club-level decisions)
- Data-driven hot takes (a stat that contradicts the popular narrative)
