# Channel Profile: @davidkingstories

## 0. THE NON-NEGOTIABLE FORMULA

**THE OPENING FORMULA — "As [subject] [action]..." (the mid-action cold open)**

The single most defining trait. **~72% of videos (18 of 25)** open with the literal word **"As"**, dropping the viewer into a physical action already in progress, mid-sentence, with zero context.

Verbatim first lines:
- "**As** they attacked the ball, the players smashed into each other and he hit the ground clutching at his face."
- "**As** he lined up to take the penalty kick, his own manager was screaming at him from the sideline."
- "**As** a US player crossed the ball into the centre, the New Zealand defender tried desperately to intercept it."
- "**As** Brazil lined up a free kick, the silly Zaire defender burst from the wall, sprinted at the ball and just booted it away."
- "**As** the cannibal plunged his studs straight into the leg of Ronaldo, he hit the ground wincing in pain..."
- "**As** the Brazilian players in their yellow shirts jogged out onto the pitch in Pyongyang, 80,000 North Koreans rose to their feet and roared."
- "**As** Lionel Messi turned to celebrate another Argentina goal, the linesman's flag went up again."
- "**As** the man in robes climbed down from the stands and strolled straight onto the World Cup pitch, the French players genuinely had no idea who he was."
- "**As** the Italian defender threw himself into the tackle, he missed the ball entirely..."
- "**As** he rolled on the ground with blood spilling from his head, the Brazilian crowd couldn't believe what they were seeing."

The remaining videos still open on a concrete physical moment or a punchy stat, never on background:
- "He desperately stretched out his leg, he hooked the ball back across the goal..."
- "It was a terrible pass back to the goalkeeper..."
- "There were only three minutes left in the game and they were winning 2-1. So they kicked the ball into their own goal."
- "It was the first time Algeria had ever made it to the World Cup..."

**THE CLOSING FORMULA — "The dry aftermath + the ironic kicker"**

He does NOT have a fixed verbal closer (no "and that's the story of..." tagline). Instead, he ends with a **rapid-fire wrap-up of consequences** (bans, fines, rule changes, deaths, later fates), then lands on **one final ironic, deadpan, or gut-punch sentence** — often a quote or a wry twist. **~90%+ of videos** end on this "consequences → kicker" structure.

Verbatim closers:
- "He just shrugged and said, It's not my fault. I didn't miss the penalty."
- "Maradona cheekily responded, a little with the head of Maradona, and a little with the hand of God."
- "and Morshid Effendi was banned from international football for the rest of his life."
- "she made a bunch of money from her newfound attention by doing a nude photo shoot in Playboy magazine."
- "But they haven't actually qualified for a single World Cup ever since the introduction of the video assistant referee. Coincidence? Coincidence."
- "And Leonhard Kuke just stood there. And smiled at his manager."

## 1. The Hook (first 3–5 seconds)

He opens on a **visceral physical event with no names and no context**, forcing the viewer to ask "wait, what?" The names, stakes, and background arrive *after* the action lands.

**Pattern A — "As [X did the thing]..." (the in-media-res action):**
- "As they attacked the ball, the players smashed into each other and he hit the ground clutching at his face."
- "As the cannibal plunged his studs straight into the leg of Ronaldo..."
- "As he lined up to take the penalty kick, his own manager was screaming at him from the sideline."

**Pattern B — "The absurd fact stated flatly" (the confounding setup):**
- "There were only three minutes left in the game and they were winning 2-1. So they kicked the ball into their own goal."
- "It was a terrible pass back to the goalkeeper, so as the opposition took advantage of their screw-up..."
- "The American lined up for the penalty shootout, but as he just dribbled the ball towards the keeper and then kicked it past him, the rest of the football world looked on in confusion."

**Pattern C — "The withheld identity" (mystery man):**
- "As the man in robes climbed down from the stands and strolled straight onto the World Cup pitch, the French players genuinely had no idea who he was."

**What he NEVER does:**
- Never opens with a name-and-date info dump ("In 1982, West Germany played Algeria...").
- Never opens with "Did you know..." or a rhetorical question to camera.
- Never introduces himself or the channel.
- Never explains WHY something matters before showing WHAT happened.

## 2. Voice & Persona

- **Narrator type:** The wry, all-knowing storyteller-historian. He is a football fan telling you a story he clearly loves, but he's slightly amused by the absurdity of it all.
- **Emotional stance:** Warm and enthusiastic on the drama, dry and deadpan on the fallout. He can swing from genuine reverence ("Lionel Messi dropped to his knees") to schoolboy cheek ("kicked the Manchester United striker in the ding-dong").
- **Relationship to viewer:** Conspiratorial "let me let you in on this." He treats you as a fellow fan who deserves the *real* story behind the clip you've half-seen. He often anticipates your assumption and then flips it: "If you're a football fan, you probably noticed already that the goal was clearly offside, right? ... But if you thought that, you'd be wrong."
- **Confidence:** Total. He states bizarre facts flatly, as settled truth, and lets the absurdity do the work.
- **Warmth:** Real but restrained. He earns the emotional beats (Escobar, Kahn) by playing everything else with a light, ironic touch, so the sincere moments hit harder.
- **Humour register:** British, understated, faintly irreverent — "flopped onto the ground like a limp sausage," "furiously shaking his ding-dong," "the silly Zaire defender."

---

## 3. Story Structure & Beat Order

His universal arc:

1. **COLD ACTION (the hook):** A single dramatic physical moment, mid-event, unexplained. (~3–8 seconds)
2. **THE CONFUSION / MISDIRECTION:** He points out what it *looks* like, and flags that something is off. "But in reality..." / "But it didn't matter..." / "Because these players aren't Thailand's defenders. They are Thailand's attackers."
3. **THE "SEE" REWIND (context bomb):** He rewinds to explain the backstory — the stakes, the rule, the political situation, the rivalry. Almost always triggered by the word **"See,"**. This is where names, dates, and tournaments finally land.
4. **ESCALATION:** He stacks the pressure with cause-and-effect chains, often listing escalating events ("Yellow card. Yellow card. Figo headbutted Van Bommel. Yellow card."). Tension builds through short, mounting beats.
5. **THE TURN / CLIMAX:** The decisive moment — the last-kick goal, the missed penalty, the own goal, the reveal of the trick. Usually the emotional peak.
6. **THE DRY AFTERMATH:** Rapid consequences delivered flatly — fines, bans, rule changes, historical footnotes.
7. **THE KICKER:** One final ironic/wry/gut-punch line, frequently a quote.

He is a master of **the withheld reveal**: he shows you an outcome that seems settled, then rewinds to explain it was engineered, faked, or forced (Rojas's razor blade, the own-goal charade, the phantom-goal hole in the net).

---

## 4. Pacing, Length & Sentence Rhythm

- **Two clear length modes:**
  - **Micro-stories (~0:51–1:30):** Single-incident twists. ~150–250 words. Tight, one reveal, one kicker. (e.g. *The Belly of the Ball*, *One Goal to Rule Them All*, *The Hand of God*.)
  - **Full stories (~2:10–2:59):** Multi-act sagas with political/historical depth. ~450–650 words. (e.g. *The Murder of Andrés Escobar*, *North Korea vs Fake Brazil*, *Human Error*.)
- **Sentence rhythm:** Long, flowing, clause-stacked sentences for context and build-up, punctuated by **sudden short punchy sentences** at moments of impact:
  - "The goal stood. Japan won the game 2-1, and they advanced to the next round."
  - "And Grosso had a clear path to run on and take his shot. But he didn't do that. Instead, he stepped straight into the defender and he just flopped onto the ground."
- **Acceleration technique:** He speeds up with anaphora and list-stacking during chaos ("Yellow card. Van Bronckhorst tripped Deco. Yellow card. Yellow card.").
- **Deceleration technique:** He slows to short declaratives at the climax to let it land ("Andres Escobar died 45 minutes later.").
- **Speaking feel:** Even, controlled, conversational — never breathless hype. He lets the *content* be dramatic, not the delivery.

---

## 5. Emotional Techniques

- **Dramatic irony (his #1 weapon):** He tells you the hidden truth the crowd/players didn't know, then makes you watch them act on false information. "QPR are safe, they don't know it yet." / "Suarez celebrated in the tunnel as the ball sailed over the goal."
- **Misdirection then reveal:** He deliberately lets you assume the obvious ("It was humiliating"; "the goal was clearly offside") and then pulls the rug: "But hang on a minute." / "But if you thought that, you'd be wrong."
- **Suspense via stakes escalation:** He keeps raising what's at risk — a career, a nation, a life. "lose by four goals or more and none of you are coming home."
- **Empathy through fear, not sadness:** For the heavy stories, he plants dread. "But it wasn't heartbreak or devastation that you could see in the Colombian's eyes. It was fear."
- **Shock through flat delivery:** He states horrific facts calmly — the razor blade, the six gunshots, "screamed goal after every single shot" — letting the deadpan amplify the horror.
- **Payoff / catharsis:** The big emotional wins are earned by piling on the doubt first, then releasing it: "a cacophony of voices telling him he'd missed his opportunity, Lionel Messi's Argentina were finally World Cup champions."
- **Comic relief valve:** He uses cheeky humour to keep even dark stories entertaining, then snaps back to sincerity.

## 6. Signature Phrases, Transitions & Verbal Tics

1. **"See,"** — his signature context-rewind trigger. "See, in football, a ball isn't out the moment it touches the grass beyond the line." / "See, three months before this incident, Zaire had been crowned..."
2. **"But in reality..."** — the misdirection flip. "But in reality, Patrick Vieira hadn't kicked Ruud van Nisselrooy at all."
3. **"But hang on a minute."** — the mid-story reveal beat. "But hang on a minute. Michaela had just scored what's known in football as a perfect hat trick."
4. **"But if you thought that, you'd be wrong."** — direct address, subverting assumptions. "If you're a football fan, you probably noticed already... But if you thought that, you'd be wrong."
5. **"However,"** — his workhorse turn-word, always signalling a reversal. "However, when the officials that Mbutu sent along with the squad pocketed the player's bonus points..."
6. **"So [they did the absurd thing]."** — deadpan cause-and-effect delivering the punchline. "So he did what any self-respecting royal does when things don't go his way. He threw a fit."
7. **"It seemed like an absurd thing to do."** / **"It did seem a little crazy..."** — narrator acknowledging the ridiculousness on your behalf.
8. **"Coincidence? Coincidence."** — his self-answering rhetorical tic, dripping with dry sarcasm.
9. **British-cheeky euphemisms:** "kicked the Manchester United striker in the ding-dong," "furiously shaking his ding-dong," "flopped onto the ground like a limp sausage."
10. **"the most [X] ever" historical-superlative anchoring:** "the most red cards ever shown," "the fastest goal in World Cup history," "the most watched sporting event in history."
11. **"with a chip on her/their shoulder"** — recurring motivation phrase. "now, with a chip on her shoulder, she played harder than she ever had before."

## 7. Subject Matter & Angle

He tells **true football stories where something absurd, scandalous, or morally loaded happened** — and the angle is always the **hidden truth or the twist of fate behind a clip you might have seen**.

His topic sweet spots:
- **Scandals & cheating:** Rojas's razor blade, the Disgrace of Gijón, the Tiger Cup own-goal farce, the Hand of God.
- **Rule quirks & loopholes:** the golden double-goal, the 1.88mm belly of the ball, the phantom goal through a hole in the net, away-goals rule.
- **Political/historical intrusion into sport:** North Korea's fake Brazil, Zaire's dictator, cartel-owned Colombian clubs, the Kuwaiti royal tantrum.
- **Injustice & conspiracy:** South Korea 2002 "human error," the Australian dive.
- **Heroism, sacrifice & tragedy:** Trautmann's broken neck, Andrés Escobar's murder, Kahn's redemption.
- **Redemption/against-the-odds triumph:** Messi 2022, the Invincibles, Spurs' last-kick comeback.

**What makes a topic "David King":** there must be a **reveal** — a thing that isn't what it looked like — plus a **downstream consequence** (a ban, a rule change, a death, a historical first). He loves stories where the truth is stranger and darker than the clip suggests.

## 8. The Closer / Twist

He ends by **collapsing the aftermath into rapid consequences, then landing one final ironic sentence** — often a real quote, a bitter twist, or a deadpan sting. He does NOT sign off or CTA.

Closer archetypes:

- **The quote-kicker (his favourite):**
  - "He just shrugged and said, It's not my fault. I didn't miss the penalty."
  - "Maradona cheekily responded, a little with the head of Maradona, and a little with the hand of God."
  - "Moon was quoted as saying, The North Koreans would be very angry if we had won."

- **The bitter irony twist:**
  - "But they haven't actually qualified for a single World Cup ever since the introduction of the video assistant referee. Coincidence? Coincidence."
  - "Indonesia thought they'd pulled off the perfect prank... But Singapore, insulted at being branded the easy win, used it as fuel. They knocked Indonesia out 2-1, and they went on to win the entire tournament."

- **The consequences-list sting:**
  - "Stupa never refereed at a World Cup again, the Sheikh was hit with a fine from FIFA, and Kuwait, in their first and only ever World Cup appearance, lost their next game to England and went home."

- **The quiet visual-image ending:**
  - "And Leonhard Kuke just stood there. And smiled at his manager."

- **The gut-punch tragedy ending (for heavy stories), pivoting to hope:**
  - Escobar's murder closes on the foundation "keeping them off the streets and away from the cartel."

## 9. How to Write in This Style — Step by Step

1. **Open mid-action with "As..." + a physical, unexplained moment.** No names, no date, no context. Describe exactly what a viewer would SEE. Make it confusing or shocking. ("As the keeper turned his back on the free kick, the striker rolled the ball the other way...")
2. **Flag that something is off.** Within one or two sentences, point out what it looks like — and hint it isn't that. Use "But in reality..." / "But it didn't matter, because..." / "But if you thought that, you'd be wrong."
3. **Drop the context bomb with "See,".** Now rewind. Give the stakes, the rule, the rivalry, the politics. This is where names, tournaments, and dates arrive. Make the stakes life-sized ("a career," "a nation," "his family").
4. **Escalate with cause-and-effect chains.** Stack short mounting beats. Use "However," and "So" to pivot. During chaos, use list-stacking for rhythm.
5. **Deliver the turn/climax in short punchy sentences.** Slow down. Let the decisive moment breathe. ("But he didn't do that. Instead, he just flopped onto the ground.")
6. **Wrap the aftermath fast and flat.** Fines, bans, rule changes, later fates, historical firsts — rattle them off in one or two sentences.
7. **Land the kicker.** End on a real quote, a bitter irony, or a wry deadpan sting. Never explain the moral. Let the irony do it. If tempted, use a self-answering tic like "Coincidence? Coincidence."
8. **Sprinkle the voice throughout:** one or two cheeky British euphemisms, at least one direct-address flip to the viewer, and the words "See," "However," "But in reality," and "It seemed like an absurd thing to do."

**Three example opening lines in his exact voice:**

- *(On the 1990 Higuita scorpion-kick fiasco):* "As the harmless-looking ball floated toward his goal, the Colombian keeper turned his back on it, flicked his heels up over his head, and cleared it with a scorpion kick. It looked insane. But hang on a minute — this was a World Cup, and one tiny mistake here would've sent his entire nation home."

- *(On Bakayoko/Roy Keane–style feud, or a manager-player war):* "As the substitute board went up with his number on it, the striker ignored it completely, turned to his own manager, and told him exactly where to go. See, this wasn't the first time the two of them had nearly come to blows — and it wouldn't be the last."

- *(On the 1966 stolen World Cup trophy found by a dog):* "As the little black-and-white dog sniffed around under a suburban hedge, he pulled out a parcel wrapped in newspaper. Inside was the most wanted object in world football. See, a week earlier, the actual World Cup trophy had been stolen from a locked glass case, and all of England was in a panic."
