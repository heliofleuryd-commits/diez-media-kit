# Structure Guide — Analytical Football Video Scripts

How to structure analytical football content for TikTok and short-form. This guide covers video length, beat-by-beat architecture, what works, what kills engagement, and templates for each analytical format type.

---

## Video Length — What Actually Works

Analytical content has a different length sweet spot from emotional storytelling. The viewer is there to learn something, not to feel something. Their patience is conditional on forward momentum.

| Length | Use case | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **30–45s** | One-point punchy takes, single-stat reveals, quick comparisons | No room for evidence — turns into assertion without proof |
| **60–75s** | Tactical breakdowns, "why" explainers, player analysis | Sweet spot for analytical — enough for 2–3 evidence beats |
| **90–120s** | Deep dives, rankings, multi-factor analysis | Requires airtight pacing — every second must advance the argument |
| **120s+** | Series content, full career breakdowns, multi-team comparisons | Only works if established with a loyal audience; loses casual viewers fast |

**The rule:** If you cannot name exactly what the viewer learns at the 30s mark, the 60s mark, and the 90s mark — the video is too long or too loosely structured.

## The Core Analytical Architecture

All high-performing analytical content follows this beat structure regardless of length:

### Beat 1 — The Provocation (0–5s)
A bold claim, a surprising stat, or a question that frames the entire video. The viewer decides to stay here. The provocation must promise a specific payoff — not "let me tell you about X" but "here is why X is wrong / underrated / broken."

**What works:** Specificity. A real number. A named player. An exact match. "Third-best in Europe last season" beats "one of the best."
**What kills it:** Vague setup. "In today's video we'll look at..." is not a provocation — it's a menu.

### Beat 2 — The Frame (5–15s)
Establish why this matters now. Connect the topic to something current: a transfer window, an ongoing season, a debate the audience already has. Without a "why now," the analytical content feels like homework.

**What works:** A single sentence that anchors the topic to a live conversation. "With [player] linked to [club], the question of [topic] matters more than ever."
**What kills it:** Lengthy context-setting that delays the first piece of evidence. If the viewer doesn't have something new by 15 seconds, they're gone.

### Beat 3 — The Evidence Stack (15s–75% mark)
Deliver your argument in discrete, layered beats. Each beat must add a new layer — not repeat the claim with different words, but advance it. Think of it as a staircase: each step is higher than the last.

**Structure each evidence beat as:**
1. State the point (one sentence, declarative)
2. Support it (one specific stat, moment, or comparison)
3. Connect it back to the main argument (one sentence)

Then immediately move to the next beat. No lingering.

**What works:** Concrete numbers, named examples, direct comparisons. "His pressing rate drops 23% in away games" is an evidence beat. "He doesn't work hard away from home" is an assertion.
**What kills it:** Lists of stats without a connective argument. Dumping data is not analysis — it's noise. Every stat must serve the claim.

### Beat 4 — The Complication (at ~65% mark)
Introduce the strongest counter-argument or the nuance that makes your take harder to dismiss. This is what separates analytical content from hot takes: you acknowledge the opposing view and address it.

**What works:** One sentence naming the best objection, then one to two sentences explaining why it doesn't change the conclusion. This builds credibility and shows you've done the work.
**What kills it:** Ignoring counter-arguments entirely — it makes the analysis feel thin. Or spending too long on the objection — it should sharpen your conclusion, not undermine it.

### Beat 5 — The Payoff (75–90% mark)
Land the conclusion. State your original claim again, but now it's earned — the viewer has seen the evidence. Be decisive. The payoff should feel inevitable: "So the answer is X, and here's what it means."

**What works:** A restatement of the core claim that now lands harder because the evidence has been built. One sentence of implication ("which means [club] need to [action]").
**What kills it:** A soft landing. "So there are arguments on both sides..." is not a payoff — it's a forfeit. You made a claim; honour it.

### Beat 6 — The CTA / Open Question (final 5–10%)
Two options depending on your goal:
- **Engagement:** End with an open question that invites disagreement. "Which means the real question is: can [player] do it in a big tournament?" This drives comments.
- **Follow:** End with a soft category prompt. "For more breakdowns like this, follow." Never aggressive; never mid-narrative.

**Do not:** end with both a question and a follow prompt. Pick one.

## Format-Specific Templates

### The "Why" Explainer
**Central question:** Why did X happen / why does X happen?

```
[0–5s]  Provocative reframe of the "obvious" answer — then reject it
[5–15s] "The real reason is..." — name the actual argument
[15–40s] Evidence Beat 1 — the foundational cause
[40–65s] Evidence Beat 2 — the mechanism / how it plays out
[65–80s] The complication — why people don't see this
[80–95s] Payoff — "So when you see [X], you're actually watching [Y]"
[95s+]  Open question for comments
```

**What kills it:** Starting with "So a lot of people are asking why X happened." Just answer it — skip the meta-framing.

---

### The Player Analysis
**Central question:** How good is this player, and in what specific way?

```
[0–5s]  The claim — a single, precise evaluation ("He is the best X in Europe at Y")
[5–15s] Frame — why this player / why now (recent match, upcoming transfer, debate)
[15–35s] Strength evidence — one to two specific, measurable strengths
[35–55s] Limitation evidence — the honest complication (what he can't do)
[55–75s] Contextual comparison — how he measures against peers in this specific frame
[75–90s] Verdict — restate the original claim with earned confidence
[90s+]  "The only question left is..." → open for comments
```

**What kills it:** Covering everything about the player. Pick one analytical frame (pressing, link-up play, big-game performance) and go deep on that. Breadth kills analytical credibility.

---

### The Comparison / Ranking
**Central question:** Who is better / what is the right order?

```
[0–5s]  The most provocative position in your ranking — put the controversial one first
[5–12s] Why this comparison matters and why conventional wisdom is wrong
[12–35s] Criteria — name exactly what you're measuring and why it's the right metric
[35–65s] Work through the ranking with one evidence beat per player/team
[65–80s] The surprising entry — the one that challenges assumptions
[80–95s] Final verdict with a decisive stake in the ground
[95s+]  The inviting question — "who did we miss?" or "do you agree?"
```

**What kills it:** Rankings without defined criteria. If you don't say what you're measuring, you're just listing names. The criteria is the argument.

---

### The Tactical Breakdown
**Central question:** How does this system / player / team actually work?

```
[0–5s]  The counter-intuitive observation ("This team concedes fewer goals when they press higher")
[5–15s] The setup — what most people think is happening vs. what's actually happening
[15–45s] The mechanism — how it works, step by step (keep it to 3 steps maximum)
[45–70s] The evidence — one to two match examples where it played out exactly as described
[70–85s] The implication — what this means for how opponents should set up against them
[85s+]  The open question — "can they sustain this if [player] leaves?"
```

**What kills it:** Tactical jargon without translation. Define every technical term the moment you use it. One clause, immediately after: "...a high-press structure — meaning they win the ball in the opponent's half, not their own."

---

### The Deep Dive / Career Analysis
**Central question:** What is this player's / manager's / club's real story?

```
[0–5s]  The hook — one fact that reframes everything the viewer thought they knew
[5–20s] The setup — who they are and what the conventional narrative says
[20–50s] Act 1 — the peak or defining period, in detail
[50–80s] Act 2 — the complication or decline, what changed and why
[80–100s] Act 3 — the legacy reframe — what history actually shows
[100s+] "So when people say [conventional view], the numbers say something different"
```

**What kills it:** Chronological biography. Don't tell the story in order — tell it in order of impact. Start with the most interesting part, then work backwards to explain how they got there.

## What Consistently Kills Analytical Content

**1. The Opinion Without the Evidence**
Making a claim and then never proving it with specifics. "He's not as good as people think" needs a stat, a match, a comparison — or it's just noise.

**2. The Evidence Without the Argument**
Dumping stats without a connective argument. A screen full of numbers is not analysis — it's a press release. Every number must serve the central claim.

**3. Buried Lead**
Making the viewer wait more than 15 seconds for the first piece of new information. Front-load one concrete, specific fact in the first 12 seconds.

**4. False Balance**
Ending with "so both sides have a point." This is a forfeit. Make a decision. The viewer came for an answer.

**5. Over-qualification**
"It's quite complex," "there are many factors," "in some ways." These phrases signal that you don't trust your own argument. State claims directly.

**6. Jargon Without Translation**
Using tactical or statistical terms without immediate definition. Half your audience doesn't know what xG means. Define it once, confidently, and move on.

**7. The Flat Middle**
Evidence beats that feel like a list rather than a staircase. Each beat must escalate the argument, not just add another item to a list. Ask yourself: does beat 3 make beat 2 feel more powerful? If not, reorder or cut.

---

## The Single Most Important Structural Rule

**Every beat must advance the argument — not repeat it, not decorate it, not prove you've done research. Advance it.**

The viewer grants you their attention once, and you must earn it back with every sentence. If a line doesn't move the argument forward, cut it. Analytical content that earns trust is relentless: it never stalls, never repeats, and never backs away from the claim it opened with.
